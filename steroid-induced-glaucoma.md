Steroid-Induced Glaucoma: When Treatment Can Raise Eye Pressure
Steroids are important medicines. They reduce inflammation and can
protect vision in conditions such as uveitis and after eye surgery. But
in some people, steroids can raise eye pressure. If that pressure rise
damages the optic nerve, it can lead to steroid-induced glaucoma.
The difficulty is that raised eye pressure usually causes no symptoms.
You may feel that your eyes are fine while damage is developing.
The answer is not to avoid steroids altogether. It is to use them for
the right reason, at the right dose and duration, with appropriate
monitoring.
Key Takeaways
Steroid-induced glaucoma is a form of secondary glaucoma associated
with corticosteroid use.
Steroids can raise eye pressure even when they are prescribed for a
genuine medical reason.
The risk is not limited to eye drops. Steroids given around or
inside the eye, and some medicines taken by mouth, inhaled, injected
or applied to the skin, may also affect eye pressure.
A pressure rise often has no warning symptoms. Measuring eye
pressure is the only way to detect it reliably.
Steroid-related raised eye pressure does not always mean that
glaucoma has developed. Glaucoma means that the optic nerve has
sustained damage.
Do not start, stop or change prescribed steroids on your own. Speak
to the doctor managing the condition and arrange an eye-pressure
assessment when appropriate.
What Is Steroid-Induced Glaucoma?
Steroid-induced glaucoma occurs when corticosteroid treatment raises
intraocular pressure (IOP) and the pressure rise leads to damage of the
optic nerve.
It is useful to distinguish two related terms:
Steroid-induced ocular hypertension: Eye pressure rises after
steroid exposure, but there is no established glaucomatous optic
nerve damage.
Steroid-induced glaucoma: The raised pressure is associated with
damage to the optic nerve, which can cause permanent loss of vision.
Not everyone who responds to steroids develops glaucoma. Some people
have a rise in pressure that settles after the steroid is reduced or
stopped under medical supervision. Others need pressure-lowering
treatment, sometimes for longer.
The distinction matters because the pressure reading alone does not tell
us whether the optic nerve is safe.
How Do Steroids Raise Eye Pressure?
The front of the eye contains a clear fluid called aqueous humour. It is
continuously produced and drains through a specialised tissue called the
trabecular meshwork.
Steroids can change the way this drainage tissue functions. In
susceptible people, resistance to the outflow of aqueous humour
increases. Fluid then drains less efficiently, and pressure inside the
eye can rise.
This is usually an open-angle mechanism: the drainage angle may remain
anatomically open, but the drainage system does not work as well.
The response varies from person to person. The type of steroid, its
potency, dose, frequency, route of administration and duration of use
all influence risk. Individual susceptibility matters too.
Which Steroids Can Cause Glaucoma?
The risk is best recognised with steroid eye drops, but exposure can
occur in several forms.
Eye drops and eye ointments: These are common causes,
particularly when used frequently or for prolonged periods.
Injections around or inside the eye: Periocular and intravitreal
steroid treatments can produce a significant pressure rise in some
patients.
Oral or injected systemic steroids: These may raise eye pressure
in susceptible individuals, although the risk varies with the
medicine and exposure.
Inhaled or nasal steroids: These are generally used at lower
systemic exposure, but a pressure response can occur in susceptible
people, especially with prolonged treatment.
Steroid creams applied to the eyelids or face: Repeated use near
the eyes can be relevant and should not be overlooked.
Examples of ophthalmic corticosteroids include prednisolone acetate,
dexamethasone and difluprednate. This is not a list of medicines to
avoid. These drugs can be necessary and effective. The important
question is whether they are appropriate for your condition and whether
your eyes need monitoring while you use them.
Combination drops used for red eyes may contain both an antibiotic and a
steroid. The presence of an antibiotic does not remove the
pressure-related risk from the steroid component.
Who Is More Likely to Develop a Steroid Response?
Some people are more susceptible than others. Risk is higher in people
with:
Existing glaucoma or ocular hypertension.
A family history of glaucoma, particularly primary open-angle
glaucoma.
A previous documented rise in eye pressure after steroid use.
High myopia in some settings.
Certain systemic conditions, including diabetes, which may be
relevant to overall risk.
A history of prolonged or repeated steroid exposure.
Children can develop a pressure rise more quickly and sometimes more
markedly than adults. They may not be able to describe symptoms clearly,
so monitoring is especially important when steroids are prescribed.
A previous uneventful course of steroid treatment does not guarantee
that a future course will be safe. The response can differ with the
preparation, dose, duration and clinical circumstances.
How Soon Can Eye Pressure Rise?
With topical steroid eye drops, a pressure rise is often detected after
a few weeks of treatment. A commonly described window is around three to
six weeks, but this is not a rule.
Some people respond earlier. Others develop a rise later, particularly
with prolonged exposure. Steroid injections may have a different time
course, and pressure elevation after an injection can persist.
This is why there is no single monitoring schedule that is right for
every patient. The plan should take account of the steroid being used,
the reason for treatment, the expected duration and the patient's risk
factors.
Does Steroid-Induced Glaucoma Have Symptoms?
Often, it does not.
Raised eye pressure is usually painless. You may not notice blurred
vision, discomfort or any change in your day-to-day sight while the
pressure is elevated.
If optic nerve damage develops, peripheral vision may be affected first.
This can go unnoticed because the brain compensates for gradual changes.
Central vision may remain good until disease is advanced.
Do not wait for symptoms before getting your eye pressure checked if you
have been advised to monitor it.
Severe eye pain, a red eye, sudden blurred vision, halos around lights,
headache, nausea or vomiting need urgent medical assessment. These
symptoms can have several causes and should not automatically be
attributed to steroid use.
How Is Steroid-Induced Glaucoma Diagnosed?
A pressure measurement is an important starting point, but it is not the
whole diagnosis.
An ophthalmologist may assess:
Medication history: Which steroid is being used, by which route,
at what dose and for how long? The doctor should also know about
creams, inhalers, nasal sprays and injections.
Intraocular pressure: Eye pressure is measured, often using
applanation tonometry. A previous baseline reading is helpful when
available.
Optic nerve examination: The optic nerve is examined for signs
of glaucomatous damage.
Optical coherence tomography (OCT): When indicated and
available, OCT can assess the retinal nerve fibre layer and ganglion
cell complex.
Visual field testing: This looks for functional loss that may
not be obvious from central vision or a routine eye examination.
Gonioscopy and other examinations: These may help assess the
drainage angle and identify other possible causes of raised
pressure.
The diagnosis requires clinical judgement. A high pressure reading
during steroid treatment may represent steroid-induced ocular
hypertension, pre-existing glaucoma, another form of secondary glaucoma,
or more than one process at the same time.
A pressure rise should not automatically be blamed on steroids without
considering the wider clinical picture.
What Happens If Steroid-Induced Glaucoma Is Not Treated?
Persistently raised eye pressure can damage the optic nerve. Once
glaucomatous nerve damage has occurred, it is generally irreversible.
The amount of damage depends on factors such as the pressure level,
duration of exposure, the optic nerve's susceptibility and whether
glaucoma was already present.
The good news is that an early steroid response can often be managed
effectively. Detecting a pressure rise before significant nerve damage
occurs gives the treating team more options.
Normal vision today does not, by itself, prove that the optic nerve is
safe.
How Is Steroid-Induced Glaucoma Treated?
Treatment depends on why the steroid is needed, how high the pressure
is, whether the optic nerve is damaged and whether the pressure remains
elevated.
1. Review the steroid treatment
If it is medically safe, the prescribing doctor may reduce the dose,
shorten the course, stop the steroid or consider an alternative with a
lower tendency to raise eye pressure.
This decision must be made with the doctor treating the underlying
condition. Abruptly stopping steroids can be harmful in some situations,
including certain inflammatory eye diseases and systemic illnesses.
2. Lower the eye pressure
If pressure remains high or the optic nerve is at risk,
pressure-lowering eye drops may be needed. The choice depends on the
patient's eye findings, medical history and other medicines.
Sometimes pressure-lowering treatment is required while the steroid is
being reduced, because the pressure may not fall immediately.
3. Consider laser or surgery when needed
If pressure cannot be controlled adequately with medication and
appropriate changes to steroid treatment, laser or glaucoma surgery may
be considered. This is not necessary for everyone with a steroid
response.
4. Continue monitoring
A single normal pressure reading does not always settle the question.
Follow-up may be needed to confirm that pressure has remained controlled
and that the optic nerve and visual fields are stable.
Some patients return to their previous pressure after steroid
withdrawal. Others continue to need glaucoma treatment. The outcome
depends on the individual case.
Can Steroid-Induced Glaucoma Be Prevented?
The risk can often be reduced, although it cannot always be eliminated.
Use steroid medicines only for a clear medical indication and as
directed.
Avoid repeatedly using leftover steroid eye drops for redness,
irritation or an undiagnosed eye problem.
Tell every treating doctor if you have glaucoma, ocular hypertension
or a previous steroid response.
Ask whether and when your eye pressure should be checked if you are
starting a prolonged course of steroids.
Keep follow-up appointments, particularly if you are using steroid
drops for several weeks or receiving steroid injections around or
inside the eye.
Do not assume that a cream, inhaler or nasal spray is irrelevant to
your eye history. Mention all steroid exposure during your
consultation.
In India, steroid-containing combination eye drops may be available
through pharmacies and are sometimes used repeatedly without an
examination. A red eye can be caused by allergy, infection,
inflammation, dry eye or other conditions. These problems do not all
need the same treatment. Using a steroid without the correct diagnosis
can delay appropriate care and may cause avoidable harm.
The solution is not to fear every steroid prescription. It is to avoid
unsupervised use and make monitoring part of treatment when needed.
Frequently Asked Questions
Can steroid eye drops cause glaucoma?
Yes. Steroid eye drops can raise eye pressure in susceptible people. If
the pressure rise leads to optic nerve damage, it can cause
steroid-induced glaucoma. Risk depends on the preparation, dose,
duration and individual susceptibility.
Can I get steroid-induced glaucoma even if my vision is normal?
Yes. Raised eye pressure usually has no symptoms, and early glaucoma may
not affect central vision. An eye-pressure measurement and appropriate
optic nerve assessment are more reliable than judging by how well you
can see.
How long can I use steroid eye drops safely?
There is no single duration that is safe for everyone. Some patients
need steroids for a short course; others require longer treatment for
significant inflammation or after surgery. The prescribing
ophthalmologist should decide the duration and whether pressure
monitoring is needed.
Will my eye pressure return to normal after I stop steroids?
It often falls after the steroid is reduced or stopped, when that change
is medically appropriate. The time taken varies. After prolonged
exposure or steroid injections, pressure may remain elevated for longer,
and some patients need ongoing glaucoma treatment.
Do not stop a prescribed steroid without consulting the doctor managing
the condition.
Are steroid tablets, inhalers and nasal sprays also a risk?
They can be associated with raised eye pressure in some people. The risk
is not the same for every route or dose, and these medicines should not
be stopped without medical advice. Tell your ophthalmologist about all
steroid medicines you use.
Can steroid-induced glaucoma be cured forever?
A steroid-related pressure rise may resolve when the exposure is
appropriately reduced or stopped. However, optic nerve damage caused by
glaucoma is generally permanent. The priority is to identify the
pressure rise early, protect the optic nerve and maintain control over
time.
Should everyone using steroid eye drops see a glaucoma specialist?
Not everyone needs a glaucoma specialist. The need for monitoring
depends on the medicine, the duration of treatment and personal risk
factors. If you have known glaucoma, a previous steroid response, a
strong family history or a prolonged course of treatment, ask your
ophthalmologist about an appropriate monitoring plan.
My Perspective
Steroids are neither inherently dangerous nor harmless. They are
valuable medicines, and in some eye conditions withholding them can
itself threaten vision.
The clinical question is not simply, "Is this patient using a steroid?"
It is, "Why is the steroid needed, what is the patient's risk of a
pressure response, and how will we know if that response occurs?"
A pressure reading is one part of the answer. The optic nerve, visual
fields, treatment history and the condition being treated all matter.
The aim is to control inflammation without overlooking a preventable
cause of optic nerve damage. That requires appropriate prescribing,
clear communication and follow-up---not fear, and not unsupervised use.
When Should You Seek an Eye Examination?
Arrange an ophthalmic review if you are using steroid eye drops for
longer than advised, have been using leftover drops repeatedly, have
glaucoma or a previous steroid response, or have been told that your eye
pressure is elevated.
Seek urgent care for sudden changes in vision, severe eye pain, a red
eye with halos, or eye symptoms accompanied by headache, nausea or
vomiting.
If you are unsure whether a steroid has affected your eye pressure,
bring the medicine or a photograph of its label to your appointment. The
name, strength, frequency and duration are useful information.
