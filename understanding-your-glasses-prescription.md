# Understanding Your Glasses Prescription

> **A glasses prescription is not a measure of how healthy your eyes are. It is simply a description of how light should be focused to give you the clearest possible vision.**

Many patients receive a spectacle prescription filled with numbers and abbreviations that seem impossible to understand.

Questions such as:

- What does +2.00 mean?
- What does -4.50 mean?
- What is CYL?
- What is AXIS?
- What is ADD?
- Why has my prescription changed?
- Do stronger glasses mean my eyes are getting weaker?

are extremely common.

This guide explains what your prescription means and what it does not mean.

---

# What Is a Glasses Prescription?

A spectacle prescription tells the optician how lenses should bend light so that it focuses correctly on your retina.

It does **not** diagnose eye disease.

It simply corrects blurred vision caused by refractive errors.

---

# Common Types of Refractive Error

## Myopia (Short-Sightedness)

People with myopia see nearby objects clearly but distant objects appear blurred.

The prescription usually begins with a **minus (-)** number.

Example:

- -1.50
- -3.00
- -6.25

---

## Hyperopia (Long-Sightedness)

People with hyperopia may have difficulty focusing on nearby objects.

The prescription usually begins with a **plus (+)** number.

Example:

- +1.00
- +2.50

---

## Astigmatism

Astigmatism occurs when the front surface of the eye is not perfectly round.

Instead of one clear focus, light focuses at different points.

Astigmatism is very common and completely normal.

---

## Presbyopia

As we age, the natural lens gradually loses its ability to focus on nearby objects.

This usually begins after the age of 40.

Reading glasses or multifocal lenses help compensate for this normal ageing change.

---

# Understanding the Prescription

A typical prescription may look like:

| Eye | SPH | CYL | AXIS |
|------|----:|----:|-----:|
| Right | -2.00 | -0.75 | 180 |
| Left | -1.75 | -1.00 | 170 |

Each part has a different meaning.

---

# Sphere (SPH)

The sphere indicates the amount of short-sightedness or long-sightedness.

Minus (-) numbers indicate myopia.

Plus (+) numbers indicate hyperopia.

The larger the number, the stronger the lens required.

---

# Cylinder (CYL)

Cylinder measures the amount of astigmatism.

If this box is blank or zero, there is little or no astigmatism.

---

# Axis

Axis describes the orientation of astigmatism.

It is measured in degrees from 1 to 180.

The axis does **not** indicate severity.

It simply tells the laboratory how to position the cylindrical correction.

---

# Addition (ADD)

ADD is used for reading glasses, bifocals and progressive lenses.

It represents the extra focusing power needed for near vision.

---

# Prism

Some prescriptions include prism.

Prism helps align images seen by the two eyes and may be prescribed for certain forms of double vision or eye muscle imbalance.

Not everyone needs prism correction.

---

# Pupillary Distance (PD)

PD measures the distance between the centres of your pupils.

It helps ensure that the optical centre of each lens is correctly aligned with your eyes.

---

# Why Does My Prescription Change?

Changes may occur because of:

- Normal ageing
- Myopia progression
- Cataracts
- Diabetes
- Dry eye disease
- Eye surgery
- Natural variation in testing

Small changes are common and usually expected.

---

# Does a Stronger Prescription Mean My Eyes Are Getting Worse?

Not necessarily.

A change in prescription does **not** automatically mean eye disease.

Sometimes the eye's focusing system changes naturally over time.

Sometimes a change reflects cataracts or another treatable condition.

Regular eye examinations help identify the cause.

---

# Will Wearing Glasses Make My Eyes Weaker?

No.

This is one of the most common myths.

Glasses improve vision.

They do **not** weaken the eyes or make eyesight dependent on spectacles.

---

# Why Doesn't My New Prescription Feel Right?

It is common to need a few days to adapt to new glasses.

This is especially true if:

- the prescription has changed significantly
- astigmatism has been corrected for the first time
- you are wearing progressive lenses
- prism has been prescribed

Persistent discomfort should be discussed with your optician or ophthalmologist.

---

# Why Can Two Prescriptions Be Different?

Different examinations may produce slightly different results.

Reasons include:

- Dry eyes
- Fatigue
- Time of day
- Differences in testing technique
- Small normal variations

Your ophthalmologist considers the overall picture rather than focusing on tiny differences.

---

# Can Glasses Prevent Eye Disease?

No.

Glasses improve vision.

They do not prevent glaucoma, cataracts or retinal disease.

Regular eye examinations remain essential, even if your vision seems excellent with glasses.

---

# Contact Lenses and Glasses

Contact lens prescriptions are **not** the same as spectacle prescriptions.

Contact lenses sit directly on the eye and require additional measurements such as:

- Base curve
- Diameter
- Lens material

Never use a spectacle prescription to order contact lenses.

---

# My Philosophy

A spectacle prescription is an important tool—but it is not the most important part of your eye examination.

My priority is not simply helping you see the smallest letters on the chart.

It is ensuring that your eyes are healthy.

Clear vision and healthy eyes are not always the same thing.

The best eye examination improves both.

---

# Frequently Asked Questions

## What does minus (-) mean?

It indicates short-sightedness (myopia).

---

## What does plus (+) mean?

It indicates long-sightedness (hyperopia).

---

## Is astigmatism serious?

Usually not.

It is extremely common and is easily corrected with glasses or contact lenses.

---

## Why do I suddenly need reading glasses?

This is usually due to presbyopia, a normal age-related change in focusing.

---

## How often should my prescription be checked?

Most adults benefit from regular comprehensive eye examinations every one to two years, or sooner if advised by their ophthalmologist.

---

# Key Take-Home Messages

- A glasses prescription corrects blurred vision but does not diagnose eye disease.
- Minus numbers indicate myopia; plus numbers indicate hyperopia.
- Astigmatism is common and usually easily corrected.
- Small prescription changes are normal.
- Wearing glasses does not weaken the eyes.
- Regular eye examinations are important even if your glasses provide clear vision.
