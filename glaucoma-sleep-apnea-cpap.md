Glaucoma, Sleep Apnea and CPAP: What Is the Connection?
Sleep apnea and glaucoma can occur in the same person, but the
relationship between them is not straightforward. Obstructive sleep
apnea may be associated with glaucoma, and some studies suggest that it
may affect the optic nerve through changes in oxygen levels, blood flow
and other mechanisms.
CPAP is an important treatment for obstructive sleep apnea. If you have
glaucoma, that does not automatically mean you should stop using it. The
decision needs to consider both your sleep apnea and your eye health.
What Is Obstructive Sleep Apnea?
Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a condition in which the upper airway
repeatedly narrows or closes during sleep. Breathing may stop briefly or
become reduced, leading to repeated drops in oxygen levels and
interruptions in normal sleep.
Common symptoms include loud snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing,
waking up gasping, morning headaches and daytime sleepiness. Not
everyone has obvious symptoms, and a sleep study is often needed to
establish the diagnosis.
OSA is more than a sleep problem. It can affect cardiovascular and
metabolic health, and it may be relevant to eye health too.
Is Sleep Apnea Linked to Glaucoma?
Research has found an association between obstructive sleep apnea and
glaucoma, particularly primary open-angle glaucoma. However, an
association does not prove that sleep apnea directly causes glaucoma in
every patient.
There are several possible explanations for a relationship:
Repeated drops in oxygen levels during sleep may affect tissues that
depend on a steady oxygen supply, including the optic nerve.
Changes in blood pressure and blood flow during sleep may affect
circulation to the optic nerve.
Sleep apnea may coexist with other health factors that also
influence glaucoma risk.
The evidence is still evolving. Not everyone with sleep apnea develops
glaucoma, and not everyone with glaucoma has sleep apnea.
The practical point is that sleep apnea belongs in the medical history
of a patient with glaucoma, especially when there are other reasons to
suspect it.
Can Sleep Apnea Make Glaucoma Worse?
It may be a relevant risk factor, but we cannot predict an individual
patient's glaucoma course from a diagnosis of sleep apnea alone.
Glaucoma is influenced by several factors, including intraocular
pressure, optic nerve susceptibility, age, family history and the
pattern of damage over time. Sleep-related oxygen changes and blood-flow
changes may also be relevant in some people.
If you have both conditions, the most useful approach is to manage each
one properly and ensure that your glaucoma follow-up is based on your
own pressure measurements, optic nerve findings and visual field
results.
Do not assume that every change in glaucoma is caused by sleep apnea.
Equally, do not ignore sleep symptoms simply because your eye pressure
is controlled.
What Is CPAP?
Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is a common treatment for
obstructive sleep apnea. A machine delivers air through a mask to help
keep the upper airway open during sleep.
For many people, CPAP reduces breathing interruptions and improves
sleep-related symptoms. It may also help reduce the broader health
consequences of untreated sleep apnea.
CPAP is not an optional comfort measure for everyone. For people with
clinically significant OSA, it may be an important part of treatment.
Can CPAP Raise Eye Pressure?
This is an important question, and the answer needs some nuance.
Intraocular pressure varies naturally over the day and night. Studies
have reported that CPAP use may be associated with changes in eye
pressure in some circumstances, including during sleep. The size and
clinical significance of these changes vary, and the available evidence
does not establish that CPAP causes glaucoma progression in every person
who uses it.
Several factors make the question difficult to answer simply. Eye
pressure is harder to measure during sleep than during a routine clinic
visit. Studies differ in design, duration and the patients included. A
pressure change observed during CPAP use does not automatically mean
that the optic nerve will be damaged.
For someone who has glaucoma, the question is therefore not just whether
CPAP can influence eye pressure. It is whether that effect is clinically
important in that particular patient.
Should You Stop CPAP If You Have Glaucoma?
No---not on your own.
Untreated obstructive sleep apnea can have significant consequences for
health and daytime functioning. Stopping CPAP without a plan may allow
sleep apnea to return or worsen.
If you have glaucoma and use CPAP, tell both your ophthalmologist and
your sleep physician. They can consider the severity of your sleep
apnea, the status of your glaucoma, your pressure measurements and
whether any additional monitoring is appropriate.
Do not change your CPAP pressure settings or stop treatment without
discussing it with the clinician managing your sleep apnea.
What If I Have Glaucoma and Use CPAP?
There is no single monitoring plan that suits every patient. The
approach depends on the type and severity of glaucoma, how stable it has
been, the severity of sleep apnea and the treatment being used.
Your ophthalmologist may consider:
Reviewing your glaucoma history and previous eye-pressure
measurements.
Checking whether your optic nerve examination, OCT and visual fields
are stable over time, when these tests are indicated and available.
Reviewing the timing and pattern of your eye-pressure measurements
if there is a clinical reason for concern.
Coordinating with your sleep physician if your glaucoma appears to
be progressing despite apparently adequate control of conventional
risk factors.
A normal pressure reading at one appointment does not tell the whole
story. Glaucoma management depends on the pattern over time, not a
single number.
At the same time, CPAP use alone is not a reason to assume that glaucoma
is progressing or to change effective glaucoma treatment without
evidence.
Does Everyone With Glaucoma Need a Sleep Study?
No. Glaucoma by itself does not mean that every patient needs testing
for sleep apnea.
However, it is worth discussing sleep apnea with your doctor if you have
symptoms such as loud habitual snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing,
waking up gasping or significant daytime sleepiness. A sleep physician
can assess whether a sleep study is appropriate.
The threshold to investigate may also be lower when a patient has other
recognised risk factors for OSA or when the clinical history raises a
specific concern. The decision should be individualised rather than
based on glaucoma alone.
Frequently Asked Questions
Does sleep apnea cause glaucoma?
Sleep apnea has been associated with glaucoma in research, but the
association does not prove direct causation in every patient. The
relationship is likely complex and may involve oxygen levels, blood flow
and other health factors.
Can CPAP worsen glaucoma?
CPAP may affect intraocular pressure in some circumstances, but the
evidence is not sufficient to conclude that it worsens glaucoma in every
patient. If you have glaucoma, discuss your situation with your
ophthalmologist and sleep physician rather than stopping CPAP.
Should I tell my ophthalmologist that I use CPAP?
Yes. Tell your ophthalmologist if you have diagnosed sleep apnea and use
CPAP. It is relevant medical information, particularly if your glaucoma
requires close monitoring or appears to be progressing.
Can I change my CPAP pressure to protect my eyes?
Do not adjust the pressure settings yourself. Your sleep physician
should determine the settings needed to treat your sleep apnea. If there
is concern about your eyes, the sleep physician and ophthalmologist can
discuss the safest approach together.
If my eye pressure is normal, can sleep apnea still matter?
It can be relevant to the overall assessment, but a normal pressure
reading does not establish whether glaucoma is stable. Optic nerve
examination, visual fields and other tests, when indicated, help assess
whether damage is changing over time.
Should I stop CPAP before an eye-pressure check?
Do not change your usual treatment just to prepare for a routine
appointment unless your treating clinician specifically advises you to.
Tell the ophthalmologist that you use CPAP and follow their instructions
about any additional assessment.
My Perspective
Glaucoma, sleep apnea and CPAP are connected by questions that are
important but not yet fully settled. Sleep apnea may be associated with
glaucoma, and CPAP may influence eye pressure in some circumstances.
Neither statement means that CPAP should routinely be stopped in a
patient with glaucoma.
The right response is to avoid both extremes: dismissing the possible
connection and assuming that CPAP is damaging the optic nerve.
If you have both conditions, make sure your ophthalmologist knows about
your sleep apnea and your sleep physician knows about your glaucoma.
Continue the treatment prescribed for each condition, and review any
concerns together.
The aim is to protect the optic nerve without leaving sleep apnea
untreated. That requires individual assessment, sensible monitoring and
coordination between the clinicians involved in your care.
When Should You Seek Advice?
Arrange an eye review if you have glaucoma and have concerns about your
CPAP treatment, particularly if your glaucoma appears to be changing
despite regular care. Discuss symptoms of sleep apnea with your doctor,
even if your eye pressure is currently controlled.
Do not stop CPAP, change its settings or alter glaucoma medication
without advice from the clinician responsible for that treatment.
