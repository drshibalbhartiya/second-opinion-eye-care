# Why Progression Requires a Repeatable Visual Field Defect

One of the commonest mistakes in glaucoma management is reacting to a single visual field that appears worse than previous tests.

Visual fields are psychophysical tests.

Unlike OCT, they depend not only on the eye, but also on the patient's attention, understanding, fatigue, motivation, and response reliability.

A patient who is tired, anxious, distracted, uncomfortable, or unfamiliar with the test may appear to have progressed when they have not.

## One Test Rarely Tells the Whole Story

Every visual field contains variability.

False positives, false negatives, fixation losses, learning effects, and simple day-to-day fluctuations can all influence the result.

This is particularly true in patients performing the test for the first few times.

Not every new defect represents disease progression.

Sometimes it simply represents test variability.

## Why Repeatability Matters

A visual field defect becomes clinically meaningful when it is reproducible.

If a suspicious change appears, my first question is rarely,

> "Has the glaucoma progressed?"

It is,

> "Can this finding be reproduced?"

Repeating the test helps distinguish true progression from random variability.

Without repeatability, there is a real risk of escalating treatment unnecessarily.

## Clinical Decisions Should Not Depend on a Single Test

Treatment decisions in glaucoma are rarely based on one visual field alone.

Instead, I consider:

- Previous visual fields
- OCT trends
- Intraocular pressure over time
- Optic nerve appearance
- Clinical examination
- The patient's overall risk profile

Progression is established by a pattern, not by an isolated outlier.

## My Perspective

Visual fields measure human performance as much as visual function.

Patients have good days and bad days.

So do visual fields.

Before concluding that glaucoma has progressed, I want to see evidence that is consistent, repeatable, and supported by the rest of the clinical picture.

Good glaucoma care is built on recognising trends—not reacting to single measurements.
