
# The S.L.O.W.™ Framework for Myopia Control: A Structured Approach to Slowing Childhood Myopia

A child whose spectacle number keeps increasing needs more than a stronger pair of glasses. Progressive myopia is associated with elongation of the eyeball, and greater degrees of myopia increase the risk of sight-threatening complications later in life.

Myopia control is therefore not simply about correcting blurred distance vision. It is about understanding why the eye is growing, identifying children at higher risk of progression and choosing interventions that can slow that growth.

At my Paediatric Myopia Control Clinic in Gurgaon, I use the S.L.O.W.™ Framework, a structured methodology I developed to bring together clinical assessment, individual risk profiling, optical interventions and pharmacological and environmental management.

The objective is simple: to move from repeatedly changing spectacle prescriptions to actively managing the progression of myopia.

## Why Is Traditional Myopia Management Not Enough?

For many years, childhood myopia was managed primarily by prescribing stronger spectacles whenever a child's vision deteriorated.

This corrects the refractive error, but standard single-vision glasses do not actively control the underlying elongation of the eye.

Axial elongation is particularly important during childhood and adolescence. An eye that becomes progressively longer is more likely to develop high myopia, which is associated with an increased lifetime risk of retinal detachment, myopic maculopathy and glaucoma.

The age at which myopia begins, the rate of progression, parental myopia and the child's individual ocular characteristics all influence future risk.

This is why myopia management needs to begin with a proper assessment rather than simply waiting for the next change in spectacle power.

## What Is the S.L.O.W.™ Myopia Control Framework?

The S.L.O.W.™ Framework is a four-step methodology devised by Dr Shibal Bhartiya for the structured assessment and management of childhood myopia.

It integrates cycloplegic refraction, axial length measurement, lifetime risk assessment, evidence-based optical interventions and appropriate pharmacological and environmental strategies.

Each step addresses a different clinical question.

- **S – Structural Baseline Mapping:** Is the eye actually growing, and what is the child's true refractive status?
- **L – Lifetime Risk & Ethnicity Profiling:** Why might this child be at greater risk of progressive or high myopia?
- **O – Optical Intervention Design:** Which optical intervention is appropriate for this child's age, prescription and progression?
- **W – Wet & World Shield:** How can pharmacological treatment and environmental changes work together to slow progression?

Together, these steps create an individualised myopia management plan rather than a one-size-fits-all prescription.

## Step 1: S – Structural Baseline Mapping

### Measuring true eye growth, not just the spectacle number

The first step is to understand the child's actual refractive status and ocular dimensions.

A spectacle prescription alone does not tell us whether the eye is continuing to elongate. Refraction can also be influenced by accommodation, particularly in children.

The structural baseline assessment includes:

**1. Cycloplegic refraction**

Cycloplegic refraction temporarily relaxes the focusing mechanism of the eye using prescribed eye drops. This helps establish the child's true refractive error and reduces the influence of accommodation.

It is particularly important in children, in whom non-cycloplegic measurements may underestimate hyperopia or overestimate myopia.

**2. Axial length measurement**

Axial length is the distance from the front to the back of the eye.

It is an important measure of structural eye growth. Repeated measurements help us understand whether the eye is elongating and whether the rate of growth is changing during treatment.

I consider axial length tracking particularly valuable because a change in spectacle power does not always tell the complete story.

**3. Keratometry**

Keratometry measures the curvature of the cornea. It provides additional information about the optical characteristics of the eye and helps interpret refractive changes.

**4. Binocular vision and accommodation assessment**

The way the two eyes work together and the way the focusing system functions can influence visual comfort and the clinical assessment.

These findings help provide a more complete picture of the child's visual system.

The purpose of structural mapping is to establish a reliable baseline against which future measurements can be compared.

## Step 2: L – Lifetime Risk & Ethnicity Profiling

### Understanding why some children progress faster than others

Myopia progression is not the same in every child.

Two children with identical spectacle prescriptions may have very different risks of developing high myopia. The age of onset, family history, rate of progression and environmental factors all matter.

The S.L.O.W.™ Framework therefore includes a structured assessment of future risk.

Important factors include:

**1. Age of onset**

Children who develop myopia at a younger age have more years during which progression can occur.

Early-onset myopia deserves particular attention because these children may reach higher levels of myopia by adulthood.

**2. Parental myopia**

A family history of myopia increases the likelihood that a child will develop myopia.

Children with two myopic parents may be at particularly high risk. The degree of parental myopia and the age at which it developed are useful clinical details.

**3. Ethnicity and population-related risk**

Myopia prevalence and progression patterns vary across populations.

Children of South and East Asian ancestry are an important group to consider because childhood myopia is highly prevalent in these populations.

This is not a prediction based on ethnicity alone. Ancestry and population-level patterns must be interpreted alongside the child's individual clinical findings and family history.

**4. Near-work and educational demands**

Prolonged close-up work, particularly when performed continuously with inadequate breaks, may contribute to myopia development and progression.

Reading distance, duration of near work and patterns of screen use are relevant parts of the history.

**5. Outdoor exposure**

Time spent outdoors, particularly in daylight, is associated with a lower risk of developing myopia.

Outdoor exposure should be discussed as part of every child's myopia management plan.

The purpose of this step is to identify children who may need earlier or more intensive intervention rather than waiting for significant progression to occur.

## Step 3: O – Optical Intervention Design

### Choosing the right optical strategy for the individual child

Not all spectacles are designed to control myopia.

Standard single-vision lenses correct distance vision, but specialised myopia control lenses use optical designs intended to influence peripheral retinal defocus and slow axial elongation.

The choice of intervention depends on the child's age, refractive error, progression rate, ocular health, lifestyle and ability to comply with treatment.

Options may include:

**1. DIMS lenses**

Defocus Incorporated Multiple Segments (DIMS) lenses incorporate specialised optical zones designed to provide clear central vision while introducing myopic defocus in the peripheral visual field.

**2. H.A.L.T. and Stellest lens technology**

These specialised spectacle lens designs use highly aspherical lenslet technology to create a volume of myopic defocus intended to slow eye growth.

**3. Orthokeratology**

Orthokeratology involves specially designed rigid contact lenses worn overnight to temporarily reshape the cornea.

It provides daytime freedom from spectacles and may also help slow myopia progression in appropriately selected children.

It requires careful fitting, hygiene education and regular follow-up because overnight contact lens wear carries an infection risk.

**4. Standard spectacle correction**

Some children may require conventional spectacle correction depending on their clinical circumstances. The decision to introduce a specific myopia control intervention should be based on the child's risk and progression rather than the spectacle number alone.

The aim is to select an intervention that is clinically appropriate, practical and sustainable for the child and family.

Optical correction and myopia control are related but different goals. A child needs clear, comfortable vision as well as an appropriate strategy to manage progression.

## Step 4: W – Wet & World Shield

### Combining pharmacological and environmental protection

The final step brings together two important components of myopia management: pharmacological intervention and changes in the child's visual environment.

**Wet: Low-dose atropine therapy**

Low-dose atropine is used in selected children to slow myopia progression.

Atropine is prescribed in different concentrations, and the appropriate concentration depends on the child's age, progression rate, response and tolerance.

The treatment requires monitoring for possible side effects, including light sensitivity and difficulty with near vision.

Atropine does not reverse existing myopia or shorten an elongated eyeball. Its purpose is to reduce the rate of further progression.

Treatment should be individualised, and parents should not start or change atropine concentration without an ophthalmologist's advice.

**World: Environmental and behavioural management**

The child's visual environment is an important part of long-term myopia management.

I recommend incorporating the following habits into the treatment plan:

- **Outdoor time:** Aim for approximately 90–120 minutes of outdoor activity daily, where practical, with appropriate sun protection.
- **Reading distance:** Encourage children to maintain a comfortable reading distance rather than holding books or devices very close to their eyes.
- **Reading breaks:** Encourage regular breaks during prolonged near work.
- **20-20-20 rule:** After 20 minutes of near work, look at something approximately 20 feet away for at least 20 seconds. This is a useful habit for visual comfort, although it is not a proven treatment for axial elongation.
- **Screen habits:** Avoid unnecessarily prolonged, uninterrupted near work and encourage a healthy balance between screen-based activities and outdoor play.

The Wet & World Shield recognises that myopia management cannot depend on eye drops or spectacles alone. Biological and environmental factors need to be considered together.

## How Does the S.L.O.W.™ Framework Work in Clinical Practice?

Each child undergoes an individual assessment, and the findings are brought together to create a personalised plan.

The framework can be summarised as follows:

| S.L.O.W. Step | Clinical Question | Clinical Output |
|---|---|---|
| Structural Baseline Mapping | What is the true refractive error, and is the eye elongating? | Cycloplegic refraction, axial length and baseline measurements |
| Lifetime Risk Profiling | Why might this child progress faster? | Individualised risk assessment based on age, family history and other factors |
| Optical Intervention Design | Which optical intervention is appropriate? | Personalised spectacle lens or contact lens strategy |
| Wet & World Shield | What additional interventions can help? | Individualised atropine plan, outdoor time and visual habit guidance |

The measurements are repeated during follow-up so that the response to treatment can be assessed.

Axial length tracking is particularly useful because it provides an objective measure of structural growth rather than relying only on changes in spectacle power.

## How Often Should Myopia Be Monitored?

Myopia management is a continuing process, not a one-time prescription.

A baseline assessment is followed by periodic reviews to evaluate refractive progression, axial elongation, treatment response and tolerability.

In our clinic, axial length is generally reviewed at approximately six-month intervals, with the frequency adjusted according to the child's age, progression rate and treatment plan.

A child progressing rapidly may need closer assessment.

The important question at each follow-up is not simply whether the spectacle number has changed. It is whether the eye is growing and whether the chosen intervention is adequately slowing that growth.

## Can Myopia Be Reversed?

No currently established myopia control treatment can physically shorten an eye that has already elongated.

However, appropriately selected interventions can slow the rate of further progression in many children.

This distinction is important. Myopia control is not about promising that a child's spectacle number will disappear. It is about reducing the extent of progression and potentially lowering the risk associated with high myopia later in life.

The earlier progressive myopia is identified and appropriately managed, the greater the opportunity to influence its course during the growing years.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the S.L.O.W.™ Framework?

The S.L.O.W.™ Framework is a four-step myopia control methodology developed by Dr Shibal Bhartiya. It includes Structural Baseline Mapping, Lifetime Risk & Ethnicity Profiling, Optical Intervention Design, and Wet & World Shield. It combines objective measurements, individual risk assessment, specialised optical correction, low-dose atropine where indicated and environmental management.

### At what age should myopia control begin?

Myopia control should be considered as soon as progressive myopia is identified. Children who develop myopia at an early age may have a greater opportunity for progression during their remaining growing years. Children with a strong family history may also benefit from earlier assessment.

### Are stronger glasses enough to control myopia?

No. Standard single-vision glasses correct blurred distance vision but are not designed specifically to slow axial elongation. Specialised myopia control lenses and other interventions may be appropriate depending on the child's clinical assessment.

### Why is axial length measurement important?

Axial length measures the front-to-back length of the eyeball. It helps establish whether the eye is growing and allows the ophthalmologist to monitor structural changes over time. It complements, rather than replaces, cycloplegic refraction.

### Does low-dose atropine work?

Low-dose atropine has been shown to slow myopia progression in children. The response varies, and the appropriate concentration needs to be selected and monitored by an ophthalmologist. It is often considered alongside optical and environmental interventions.

### Do myopia control spectacle lenses work?

Specialised lenses such as DIMS and Stellest are designed to slow myopia progression by modifying the pattern of defocus reaching the retina. Their suitability depends on the child's prescription, age, progression and clinical circumstances.

### Can outdoor time prevent myopia?

Greater outdoor exposure is associated with a reduced risk of developing childhood myopia. Outdoor time is an important component of healthy visual habits, although it should not replace appropriate treatment in a child who already has progressive myopia.

### Does every child with myopia need atropine?

No. Atropine is not necessary for every child. The decision depends on the age of onset, progression rate, refractive status, risk profile and other clinical findings.

### How long does myopia control treatment continue?

There is no universal treatment duration. Myopia progression often slows as children approach adulthood, but the timing varies. Treatment and monitoring should be individualised, with decisions about reducing or stopping interventions made after reviewing the child's growth pattern and clinical response.

## My Perspective

Childhood myopia deserves a more thoughtful approach than repeatedly prescribing stronger spectacles.

The spectacle number tells us how much correction a child needs today. It does not, by itself, tell us how quickly the eye is growing or what the child's future risk may be.

This is why I developed the S.L.O.W.™ Framework.

I wanted a clinical pathway that would bring together structural measurements, lifetime risk assessment, appropriate optical interventions and pharmacological and environmental management.

Not every child needs every intervention. Not every child progresses at the same rate. And no single treatment works equally well for everyone.

The aim is to understand the individual child, measure progression objectively and choose interventions based on clinical need.

Myopia control is an investment in long-term eye health. The objective is not simply to improve vision today, but to reduce the risk of sight-threatening complications in the future.

## When Should You Bring Your Child for a Myopia Assessment?

Consider a comprehensive myopia assessment if:

- Your child's spectacle number is increasing repeatedly.
- Myopia has developed at a young age.
- One or both parents have significant myopia.
- Your child spends prolonged periods on close-up activities and has limited outdoor exposure.
- Your child has been diagnosed with moderate or high myopia.
- You want to know whether specialised myopia control treatment is appropriate.

Early assessment allows us to establish a baseline and identify children who may benefit from active intervention.
