# Fundus Photography Explained

> **A photograph can capture how your eye looks today. Its greatest value comes from comparing it with photographs taken months or years later.**

Many patients wonder why their ophthalmologist takes photographs of the inside of the eye.

Questions often include:

- Why do I need photographs if my eye has already been examined?
- What can these images show?
- Are photographs better than an OCT scan?
- Can photographs detect glaucoma?
- Will I need them again?

Fundus photography provides a permanent record of the retina and optic nerve, helping your ophthalmologist monitor changes over time.

---

# What Is Fundus Photography?

Fundus photography is a method of taking high-quality colour photographs of the inside of the eye.

The photographs usually include:

- The optic nerve
- The retina
- The macula
- The retinal blood vessels

The images become part of your medical record and can be compared with future photographs.

---

# Why Is Fundus Photography Performed?

Your ophthalmologist may recommend fundus photography to:

- Document the appearance of the optic nerve
- Monitor glaucoma
- Detect diabetic retinopathy
- Monitor retinal disease
- Assess age-related macular degeneration
- Record retinal haemorrhages or other abnormalities
- Compare changes over time

Rather than relying on memory, photographs allow direct comparison between visits.

---

# What Can Fundus Photographs Show?

Fundus photographs provide valuable information about:

## The Optic Nerve

Changes in the optic nerve may suggest glaucoma or other optic nerve disorders.

Photographs help document whether its appearance remains stable.

---

## The Retina

The retina may show evidence of:

- Diabetes
- High blood pressure
- Retinal tears
- Retinal degeneration
- Inflammation
- Other retinal diseases

---

## The Macula

The macula is responsible for detailed central vision.

Photographs may reveal changes associated with:

- Age-related macular degeneration
- Diabetic macular disease
- Other retinal disorders

---

## Retinal Blood Vessels

The appearance of retinal blood vessels may provide clues about:

- Diabetes
- High blood pressure
- Vascular disease
- Inflammation

---

# Are Fundus Photographs the Same as an OCT?

No.

These investigations complement each other.

Fundus photographs show the **appearance** of the retina and optic nerve.

An OCT scan provides detailed **cross-sectional images** of these structures.

Each answers different clinical questions.

---

# Do Fundus Photographs Diagnose Glaucoma?

Not by themselves.

Photographs help document the appearance of the optic nerve.

However, diagnosing glaucoma usually also requires:

- Clinical examination
- Eye pressure measurement
- OCT
- Visual field testing
- Gonioscopy
- Assessment over time

No single test is sufficient on its own.

---

# Why Are Repeat Photographs Helpful?

Many eye diseases develop slowly.

Repeat photographs allow your ophthalmologist to compare:

- The size of the optic nerve cup
- Retinal appearance
- Blood vessels
- Areas of pigmentation
- Other subtle changes

Often, small changes become easier to recognise when two photographs are viewed side by side.

---

# Do I Need Dilating Eye Drops?

Sometimes.

Some retinal cameras can obtain good images without dilating the pupils.

However, dilation may still be recommended to improve image quality or allow a more comprehensive examination.

---

# Is Fundus Photography Safe?

Yes.

Fundus photography is a safe, non-invasive investigation.

The camera simply captures an image of the inside of the eye.

Some cameras use a bright flash of light, which may be temporarily dazzling but is not harmful.

---

# Can Artificial Intelligence Read Fundus Photographs?

Artificial intelligence is increasingly being used to assist in detecting certain eye diseases from retinal photographs.

However, AI does not replace a comprehensive eye examination.

Photographs must always be interpreted in the context of your symptoms, examination findings and other investigations.

---

# Common Myths

## "A photograph tells the whole story."

False.

It is one important investigation, but it cannot replace a comprehensive eye examination.

---

## "If my photograph is normal, my eyes are healthy."

Not always.

Some eye diseases cannot be detected from a photograph alone.

---

## "Photographs replace OCT scans."

No.

Each investigation provides different information.

---

## "I only need photographs once."

Not necessarily.

Repeat photographs are often more valuable than a single image because they help identify change over time.

---

# Questions to Ask Your Ophthalmologist

You may wish to ask:

- Why were these photographs taken?
- Has anything changed since my last visit?
- Do the photographs match my OCT and visual field?
- Will I need repeat photographs?
- What do these images tell us about my eye health?

---

# My Philosophy

Fundus photography is valuable because it allows us to document the eye objectively.

It gives us the opportunity to compare today's eye with last year's—and with the years still to come.

Like every investigation, however, it should answer a clinical question.

A photograph is not a diagnosis.

It is one piece of evidence that helps us understand your eyes more completely.

---

# Frequently Asked Questions

## Is fundus photography painful?

No.

The test is painless and non-invasive.

---

## How long does it take?

Usually only a few minutes.

---

## Will the flash damage my eyes?

No.

The bright flash may be briefly uncomfortable, but it is safe.

---

## Do I still need an eye examination?

Yes.

Photographs support—but do not replace—a comprehensive eye examination.

---

## Why do I need photographs if I already had an OCT?

Because the two tests provide different types of information.

Together, they provide a more complete assessment of your eye health.

---

# Key Take-Home Messages

- Fundus photography provides a permanent record of the retina and optic nerve.
- It is valuable for monitoring glaucoma, diabetic eye disease and retinal disorders.
- Repeat photographs are often more useful than a single image.
- Photographs complement—but do not replace—OCT scans and clinical examination.
- Every photograph should be interpreted in the context of the complete eye examination.
