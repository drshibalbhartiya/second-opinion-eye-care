# How to Read Your OCT Report

> **An OCT scan is one of the most valuable tests in modern ophthalmology—but it is not a diagnosis. It is one piece of the puzzle.**

Many patients receive an OCT report full of numbers, coloured graphs and unfamiliar abbreviations.

It is natural to wonder:

- Is my OCT normal?
- What do the green, yellow and red colours mean?
- Does a red area mean I have glaucoma?
- Why is my doctor not worried about an abnormal scan?
- Why do I need another OCT if I already had one last year?

This guide explains what an OCT measures, what your report means, and why your ophthalmologist never relies on the scan alone.

---

# What is an OCT?

OCT stands for **Optical Coherence Tomography**.

It is a painless, non-contact scan that creates detailed cross-sectional images of the retina and optic nerve.

Think of it as an "optical ultrasound" that uses light instead of sound.

The scan usually takes only a few seconds.

---

# Why is OCT Performed?

OCT helps ophthalmologists:

- Detect glaucoma
- Monitor glaucoma progression
- Assess the optic nerve
- Diagnose macular degeneration
- Detect diabetic retinal disease
- Evaluate retinal swelling
- Assess other retinal and optic nerve disorders

---

# What Does an OCT Measure in Glaucoma?

For glaucoma, OCT mainly measures the thickness of the nerve fibres and nerve cells that carry visual information from the eye to the brain.

The most commonly analysed areas include:

- Retinal Nerve Fibre Layer (RNFL)
- Ganglion Cell Layer (GCL)
- Ganglion Cell Complex (GCC) (on some machines)
- Optic nerve head

Each provides different information.

---

# What is the RNFL?

The **Retinal Nerve Fibre Layer (RNFL)** contains the nerve fibres that form the optic nerve.

Glaucoma damages these fibres over time.

A thinner RNFL may suggest glaucoma damage.

However:

- Thin RNFL does not always mean glaucoma.
- Thick RNFL does not always mean everything is normal.

Interpretation always depends on the complete clinical picture.

---

# What is Ganglion Cell Analysis?

Ganglion cells are the nerve cells that transmit visual information to the brain.

Some types of glaucoma affect these cells early.

Ganglion cell analysis may detect damage before obvious changes appear elsewhere.

---

# Understanding the Colour Codes

Most OCT reports use colours.

## 🟢 Green

Usually means the measurement falls within the machine's reference database.

Green does **not** automatically mean healthy.

---

## 🟡 Yellow

Borderline measurement.

This does not necessarily indicate disease.

Some healthy eyes fall into the borderline range.

---

## 🔴 Red

Outside the expected reference range.

Red does **not** automatically mean glaucoma.

Several factors can produce abnormal results.

---

# Can a Normal OCT Miss Glaucoma?

Yes.

Early glaucoma may occasionally appear normal on OCT.

This is why diagnosis also depends on:

- Clinical examination
- Eye pressure
- Visual field testing
- Optic nerve appearance
- Risk factors
- Follow-up over time

---

# Can an Abnormal OCT Be Wrong?

Yes.

Common reasons include:

- High myopia
- Tilted optic discs
- Poor scan quality
- Eye movement during scanning
- Cataract
- Small or unusually large optic nerves
- Anatomical variation

This is why ophthalmologists review the scan itself—not just the printed report.

---

# Why Do Different OCT Machines Give Different Results?

Different manufacturers use:

- Different software
- Different reference databases
- Different scanning protocols
- Different measurement algorithms

Results from different machines are not always directly comparable.

Whenever possible, follow-up scans should be performed on the same OCT machine.

---

# Why Do I Need Repeat OCT Scans?

One scan is only a snapshot.

Glaucoma is a disease of change over time.

Repeat scans help determine:

- Whether damage is progressing
- Whether treatment is working
- Whether findings remain stable

The trend is often more important than a single measurement.

---

# Can OCT Replace a Visual Field Test?

No.

These tests provide different information.

OCT measures **structure**.

Visual fields measure **function**.

Both are important.

---

# What If My OCT and Visual Field Don't Match?

This is common.

Examples include:

- Abnormal OCT with normal visual field
- Normal OCT with abnormal visual field

Your ophthalmologist interprets both tests together.

Neither should be interpreted in isolation.

---

# Does Everyone With Glaucoma Need OCT?

Not always.

Although OCT is extremely useful, some patients can be monitored effectively using clinical examination and visual fields.

The decision depends on the individual patient.

---

# Questions to Ask About Your OCT

Consider asking:

- Is my OCT normal?
- Has it changed since my last visit?
- Does it match my examination?
- Does it match my visual field?
- Do I need another OCT?
- Is my glaucoma stable?

Understanding these answers is often more important than understanding every number on the report.

---

# My Philosophy

An OCT scan is an excellent tool.

It is not a diagnosis.

It does not replace clinical judgement.

I never make treatment decisions based on a single scan.

Instead, I combine:

- Your symptoms
- Clinical examination
- Optic nerve assessment
- Eye pressure
- OCT
- Visual fields
- Previous test results
- Risk of future vision loss

The goal is not simply to identify abnormalities.

The goal is to understand what those abnormalities mean for **you**.

---

# Frequently Asked Questions

## Is OCT painful?

No.

The scan is completely painless and non-contact.

---

## How long does an OCT take?

Usually less than five minutes.

The scan itself takes only a few seconds.

---

## Does a red OCT mean I have glaucoma?

No.

It means the measurement falls outside the reference range.

Your ophthalmologist will interpret it in context.

---

## Can OCT detect glaucoma early?

It often helps detect structural damage early, but no test is perfect.

---

## Should I keep my OCT reports?

Yes.

Comparing scans over time is one of the most valuable ways to monitor glaucoma progression.

---

# Key Take-Home Messages

- OCT is one of the most important investigations in modern ophthalmology.
- It measures the structure of the retina and optic nerve.
- Colour codes alone do not diagnose glaucoma.
- Repeat scans are often more valuable than a single scan.
- OCT should always be interpreted alongside your examination and other tests.
