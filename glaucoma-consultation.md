What Actually Happens During a Glaucoma Consultation
A clinician's approach to diagnosing and managing glaucoma
Author: Dr Shibal Bhartiya  
Glaucoma & Neuro-Ophthalmology Specialist, Gurugram, India
---
Overview
Most people imagine a glaucoma consultation as a quick pressure check
followed by a prescription.
That is not how modern glaucoma care works.
Glaucoma is a chronic optic neuropathy that develops silently over many
years. Patients rarely notice early damage, intraocular pressure
fluctuates through the day, structural damage often appears before
functional loss, and no single investigation can confirm or exclude the
disease.
For that reason, every glaucoma consultation in my clinic follows a
structured, repeatable workflow. The sequence has evolved over years of
clinical practice and is designed to minimise diagnostic error,
establish reliable baseline measurements, and provide a framework for
monitoring progression over decades.
The objective is not to perform more investigations.
The objective is to answer the right clinical questions in the correct
order.
---
Clinical Philosophy
Every investigation should answer a specific clinical question.
Rather than treating each test as an isolated result, I combine
information from the patient's history, examination, ocular anatomy,
imaging, visual function and intraocular pressure into one clinical
picture.
Every consultation attempts to answer seven questions:
Is there structural optic nerve damage?
Is there functional visual field loss?
Do the structural and functional findings agree?
Is today's intraocular pressure sufficient to explain the findings?
Is today's pressure representative?
Could this be physiological cupping rather than glaucoma?
What personalised target pressure is most likely to prevent
progression?
Only after these questions are answered do I recommend long-term
treatment.
---
Consultation Workflow
Step 1 -- History and Baseline Vision
Every consultation begins with my optometrist.
A detailed medical and ocular history is recorded, previous
investigations are reviewed, and vision is measured as:
UCVA (uncorrected visual acuity)
Presenting vision with current spectacles
BCVA (best corrected visual acuity)
These measurements frequently explain whether reduced vision is
refractive, ocular surface related, lenticular, retinal or optic nerve
related before I begin my examination.
> **Clinical Principle**
>
> Good clinical decisions begin with good baseline data. Time invested
> before the consultation makes interpretation during the consultation
> significantly better.
---
Step 2 -- Examination
I review the history personally before examining the ocular surface,
eyelids, conjunctiva, anterior chamber and crystalline lens.
Those few minutes of conversation before the examination are
intentional.
Glaucoma is usually a lifelong relationship rather than a single
consultation.
---
Step 3 -- Structural and Functional Testing
Testing follows a deliberate sequence rather than convenience.
Depending on the patient, investigations include:
OCT
Visual field analysis
Fundus photography
Pachymetry
Goldmann applanation tonometry
Gonioscopy
The order matters because each investigation provides context for
interpreting the next.
> **Clinical Principle**
>
> Glaucoma diagnosis emerges from agreement between multiple independent
> findings rather than from one abnormal test.
---
Step 4 -- Establishing a Reliable Baseline
One pressure reading is rarely enough.
One visual field is often not enough.
Whenever appropriate I:
repeat the first visual field because of the recognised learning
effect
obtain pressure measurements at different times of day
use a water drinking test when pressure fluctuation needs further
assessment
The objective is to understand how the eye behaves rather than relying
on a single snapshot.
---
Step 5 -- Personalised Target Pressure
Modern glaucoma care no longer uses one universal "normal pressure."
Target pressure is individualised using:
optic nerve appearance
OCT findings
visual field status
corneal thickness
drainage angle anatomy
expected lifetime risk of progression
Treatment is designed to achieve a pressure likely to preserve vision
for that individual patient.
> **Clinical Principle**
>
> Intraocular pressure is the most important modifiable risk
> factor---not the disease itself.
---
Step 6 -- Eye Drop Training
Every consultation ends with practical education.
Patients are shown:
how to instil drops correctly
why technique matters
strategies to improve adherence
simple reminder systems for multiple medications
This is a core part of glaucoma care rather than an optional extra.
> **Clinical Principle**
>
> Many glaucoma treatment failures are caused by poor adherence or
> incorrect technique rather than ineffective medication.
---
Common Misconceptions
"A normal eye pressure means I don't have glaucoma."
False.
Many patients develop glaucoma despite pressures within the
statistically normal range.
---
"One visual field is enough."
Usually not.
Visual field testing has a recognised learning curve, making repeat
testing valuable when establishing a baseline.
---
"More eye drops always mean better glaucoma care."
Not necessarily.
Additional medication increases treatment burden, reduces adherence and
may worsen ocular surface disease without improving long-term outcomes.
---
How My Consultation Differs
Several aspects of my workflow differ from many routine glaucoma
consultations.
Every patient follows a structured sequence.
I personally perform Goldmann applanation tonometry before
treatment, at the first follow-up and during annual reviews.
Baselines are prioritised over isolated readings.
The first visual field is commonly repeated without charge because
learning effects are expected.
Every consultation ends with practical eye-drop education.
The emphasis is consistency rather than speed.
---
The Philosophy Behind My Clinic
Glaucoma rarely announces itself.
Patients usually feel well.
Vision often appears normal.
Pressure may even seem acceptable.
That is precisely why glaucoma care cannot rely on instinct.
It relies on method.
Every consultation follows the same structured sequence because
consistency allows subtle change to become visible over years of
follow-up.
The goal is not to collect more tests.
The goal is to collect enough reliable information that treatment
decisions remain correct not only today, but five and ten years from
now.
