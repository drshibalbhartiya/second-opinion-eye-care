# Why I Do Not "Correct" Intraocular Pressure for Central Corneal Thickness

One of the most persistent misconceptions in glaucoma is that there is a formula that can convert a measured intraocular pressure (IOP) into a patient's "true" IOP using central corneal thickness (CCT).

There is not.

## The Problem with the Bell Curve

Many clinicians are taught that normal IOP follows a Gaussian (normal) distribution, with normality defined as the mean ± two standard deviations.

In reality, the distribution of IOP is **not perfectly symmetrical**.

It is skewed towards higher pressures because glaucoma disproportionately affects the upper end of the pressure distribution. This rightward skew means that statistical definitions of "normal" cannot be interpreted as biological boundaries.

An IOP above 21 mmHg is not synonymous with glaucoma.

An IOP below 21 mmHg does not exclude glaucoma.

Pressure is only one part of the clinical picture.

## Why CCT Cannot Tell You the "True" IOP

Patients often ask whether a thin cornea means their pressure should be "corrected upwards," or a thick cornea means it should be "corrected downwards."

This is an attractive idea.

It is also unsupported.

There is no validated equation that accurately converts Goldmann applanation tonometry into a patient's true intraocular pressure simply by entering central corneal thickness.

Many correction tables have been published over the years.

None has achieved sufficient clinical validation to be used routinely.

## What Central Corneal Thickness Actually Tells Me

Central corneal thickness remains an important clinical measurement.

Not because it provides a corrected IOP.

But because it contributes to risk assessment.

A thinner cornea is associated with a higher risk of developing glaucoma and a greater likelihood of progression.

A thicker cornea is generally associated with a lower level of risk.

CCT therefore helps me interpret the patient—not recalculate the pressure.

## If I Question the Pressure Measurement

If I believe that corneal biomechanics may be significantly influencing Goldmann applanation tonometry, my next step is not to apply a correction formula.

My next step is to question the measurement technique itself.

In selected patients, Dynamic Contour Tonometry (DCT) provides an alternative method that is less influenced by corneal thickness and corneal biomechanical properties.

## My Perspective

Central corneal thickness is a risk marker.

It is **not** a calculator.

Rather than asking,

> "What should I add to this IOP because the cornea is 440 microns?"

I prefer to ask,

> "What does this corneal thickness tell me about this patient's long-term risk, and how should that influence my target pressure, follow-up, and treatment strategy?"

That question is both more clinically useful and more faithful to the available evidence.
