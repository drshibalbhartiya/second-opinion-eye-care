
# Uveitis and Raised Eye Pressure: Why Can Eye Inflammation Cause Glaucoma?

Uveitis is inflammation inside the eye. It can cause pain, redness, light sensitivity and blurred vision, but not every patient has all these symptoms. One important complication is raised intraocular pressure, which can sometimes lead to glaucoma.

The relationship is not always straightforward. In some patients, inflammation itself raises eye pressure. In others, the medicines used to control inflammation, particularly corticosteroids, are responsible. Both processes can occur in the same eye.

The challenge is to control the inflammation while protecting the optic nerve.

## What Is Uveitis?

Uveitis refers to inflammation involving the uveal tract, which includes the iris, ciliary body and choroid. Depending on the part of the eye affected, uveitis may be described as anterior, intermediate, posterior or panuveitis.

Anterior uveitis, which affects the front of the eye, is a common presentation. Patients may experience a painful red eye, light sensitivity, blurred vision or floaters. Some forms of uveitis, however, produce relatively few symptoms.

Uveitis may be associated with infections, autoimmune or inflammatory diseases, or other causes. In some patients, the underlying cause remains unidentified despite appropriate evaluation.

Identifying the type and cause of inflammation matters because treatment differs according to the clinical situation.

## Why Does Eye Pressure Rise in Uveitis?

Intraocular pressure (IOP) depends on the balance between the production and drainage of aqueous humour, the clear fluid inside the front of the eye.

Uveitis can disturb this balance in several ways.

### 1. Inflammation can obstruct fluid drainage

Inflammatory cells, proteins and debris can accumulate in the drainage system of the eye. This can interfere with the normal outflow of aqueous humour and raise eye pressure.

### 2. Inflammation can damage the drainage tissue

Persistent or recurrent inflammation may alter the trabecular meshwork, the tissue responsible for draining aqueous humour. As drainage becomes less efficient, pressure may remain elevated.

### 3. The drainage angle may become blocked

In some patients, adhesions develop between the iris and other structures in the eye. These are called synechiae. If the iris obstructs the drainage angle, eye pressure can rise substantially.

This is one reason why a patient with uveitis needs more than a pressure measurement. The doctor must also examine the structures responsible for fluid drainage.

## Can Steroid Eye Drops Raise Eye Pressure?

Yes. Corticosteroids are frequently used to control ocular inflammation, and they can be essential for protecting vision. However, some patients develop a steroid response, in which eye pressure rises during treatment.

Steroids can increase resistance to aqueous humour outflow through the trabecular meshwork. The likelihood and magnitude of the response vary between individuals and depend on factors such as the preparation, dose, duration and individual susceptibility.

This creates a clinical dilemma. Reducing inflammation is important, but prolonged or intensive steroid treatment may raise eye pressure in a susceptible patient.

The answer is not to avoid steroids whenever pressure is elevated. Untreated uveitis can itself cause serious complications. The treatment plan must address both the inflammation and the pressure.

## Is Raised Eye Pressure the Same as Glaucoma?

No.

Raised eye pressure is called ocular hypertension. It means that the pressure is elevated, but it does not automatically mean that the optic nerve has been damaged.

Glaucoma involves characteristic damage to the optic nerve, which can lead to permanent visual field loss.

In uveitis, pressure elevation may be temporary or persistent. Some patients develop glaucoma because of the pressure rise, while others may have optic nerve damage related to a combination of inflammation, pressure and other mechanisms.

A normal pressure reading at one visit does not exclude glaucoma. Equally, a high reading does not establish the diagnosis on its own.

The optic nerve and visual function need to be assessed in the context of the patient's history and examination.

## What Is Uveitic Glaucoma?

Uveitic glaucoma is glaucoma associated with inflammation inside the eye. It may develop because of the inflammatory process, changes in the drainage angle, steroid treatment or a combination of these factors.

It can be challenging to manage because the treatment for one problem may influence the other.

For example, corticosteroids may be needed to control inflammation but may also increase eye pressure. Pressure-lowering treatment may be required while inflammation is still active. In other cases, structural changes to the drainage angle make pressure control more difficult.

The management plan must therefore be individualised.

## What Symptoms Should You Watch For?

Symptoms depend on the severity of inflammation and the degree of pressure elevation.

Possible symptoms include:

- A painful or red eye.
- Light sensitivity.
- Blurred vision.
- Floaters.
- Headache or discomfort around the eye.

However, raised eye pressure may cause no symptoms at all. Some patients discover the problem only during an ophthalmic examination.

Severe eye pain, sudden deterioration in vision, marked redness, halos around lights, headache, nausea or vomiting require urgent assessment. These symptoms can indicate a serious eye problem and should not be attributed to uveitis without examination.

## How Is Uveitic Glaucoma Diagnosed?

Diagnosis requires an assessment of both inflammation and the eye's pressure-control system.

### Intraocular pressure measurement

Eye pressure is measured using an appropriate tonometry technique. Repeated measurements may be necessary because pressure can fluctuate as inflammation changes and treatment is adjusted.

### Examination for inflammation

The ophthalmologist assesses the degree and location of inflammation, including inflammatory cells in the anterior chamber when present.

### Assessment of the drainage angle

Gonioscopy allows the doctor to examine the drainage angle and identify abnormalities such as peripheral anterior synechiae or angle closure.

### Optic nerve examination

The optic nerve is assessed for evidence of glaucomatous damage. This helps distinguish raised pressure without established nerve damage from glaucoma.

### OCT and visual field testing

Optical coherence tomography (OCT), when indicated, can assess the retinal nerve fibre layer and ganglion cell complex. Visual field testing evaluates functional loss. Results must be interpreted carefully, especially during active inflammation or when other ocular complications affect vision.

The diagnosis depends on the overall clinical picture, not on a single pressure reading or test.

## How Is Raised Eye Pressure in Uveitis Treated?

Treatment depends on the cause of the pressure rise, the severity of inflammation, the drainage angle and whether the optic nerve is damaged.

### 1. Control the inflammation

Treating the underlying uveitis is often essential. Depending on the cause, treatment may include corticosteroids, other anti-inflammatory medicines, immunomodulatory treatment or antimicrobial therapy.

Infectious uveitis requires particular care because treatment appropriate for non-infectious inflammation may be inadequate or harmful if the infection is not addressed.

Steroids should not be started, stopped or changed without medical advice.

### 2. Lower eye pressure

Pressure-lowering eye drops may be required while the inflammation is treated. The choice of medication depends on the type of uveitis, the pressure level, other ocular findings and the patient's medical history.

Not every glaucoma medicine is suitable for every inflammatory eye condition. Treatment should be selected by the ophthalmologist according to the individual case.

### 3. Review steroid treatment when necessary

If a steroid response is suspected, the doctor may consider adjusting the preparation, dose or treatment strategy when it is medically safe to do so. This decision must balance the need to control inflammation against the risk of pressure-related damage.

Abruptly stopping steroid treatment can allow inflammation to worsen and may threaten vision.

### 4. Consider laser or surgery when needed

If eye pressure remains uncontrolled despite appropriate medical treatment, laser or glaucoma surgery may be considered.

The choice of procedure can be more complex in uveitic eyes. The activity of inflammation, the condition of the drainage angle and previous surgery all influence the decision. In many cases, inflammation needs to be controlled as well as possible before an elective procedure.

## Can Uveitic Glaucoma Cause Permanent Vision Loss?

Yes. Persistent pressure elevation and glaucomatous optic nerve damage can cause permanent visual field loss.

Uveitis can also affect vision through other complications, including cataract, macular oedema and damage to ocular structures. This means that reduced vision in a patient with uveitis should not automatically be attributed to glaucoma.

Early diagnosis and appropriate treatment improve the opportunity to preserve vision. Regular follow-up is particularly important in recurrent or chronic uveitis, even when symptoms have settled.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can uveitis cause glaucoma?

Yes. Uveitis can raise eye pressure through inflammation, impaired fluid drainage, changes to the drainage angle or associated steroid treatment. If glaucoma develops, the optic nerve is damaged.

### Can steroid eye drops cause raised eye pressure during uveitis treatment?

Yes. Some patients develop a steroid response. However, steroids may still be essential to control inflammation. The prescribing ophthalmologist must balance inflammation control with pressure monitoring and treatment.

### Does every patient with uveitis need glaucoma treatment?

No. Not every patient develops raised eye pressure, and not every pressure rise requires long-term glaucoma treatment. Management depends on the pressure level, its cause, the duration of elevation and whether the optic nerve has been damaged.

### Can eye pressure rise even when the uveitis seems better?

Yes. Pressure elevation may persist after inflammation improves, particularly when there are changes in the drainage system or a steroid response. Symptoms alone cannot establish whether the pressure is normal.

### Will the eye pressure return to normal?

It may, especially when the underlying cause is temporary and is treated successfully. Some patients, however, develop persistent pressure elevation or glaucoma and require ongoing treatment.

### Is uveitic glaucoma more difficult to treat?

It can be. The ophthalmologist must manage inflammation and pressure together, and some treatments for one problem may affect the other. The complexity depends on the type of uveitis, the extent of drainage damage and the status of the optic nerve.

## My Perspective

Raised eye pressure in uveitis is not a diagnosis that can be managed by looking at the pressure number alone.

The important questions are why the pressure is elevated, whether inflammation is active, whether steroid treatment is contributing, whether the drainage angle is compromised and whether the optic nerve has sustained damage.

The goal is to control the inflammatory disease while protecting the optic nerve. Sometimes this requires simultaneous treatment of inflammation and eye pressure. In other cases, the treatment strategy changes as the disease evolves.

The balance can be delicate, but avoiding treatment because of a possible steroid response is not the answer. Nor is continuing treatment without monitoring the pressure.

Careful examination, appropriate treatment and regular follow-up are central to preserving vision.

## When Should You Seek an Eye Examination?

Seek prompt ophthalmic assessment if you have uveitis with blurred vision, a painful red eye, increasing light sensitivity or a known rise in eye pressure.

If you are using steroid eye drops for uveitis, follow the monitoring schedule advised by your ophthalmologist. Do not change or stop treatment on your own.
